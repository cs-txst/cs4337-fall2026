The Pixel That Wrapped Around

A pixel holds a whole number from 0 to 255 and nothing else. Subtract two of them the obvious way and a pixel that got darker comes back looking like the brightest motion in the frame.

Two pixels

Pairs from the slide

The slide asks the class to predict print(b - a) for both pairs at once. The second pair is the one that bites.

Why it happens

NumPy keeps the result in the same uint8 type as its inputs. There is no room for a minus sign, so the arithmetic is done modulo 256: -160 becomes 96, and nothing warns you.

Promote to int16 and the sign survives. Call cv.absdiff and you get the magnitude of the change, which is what a motion detector wanted in the first place.

What this is 

    

  






  

    
The rule
Any arithmetic on two uint8 arrays stays
      uint8. Subtraction, addition and multiplication all wrap silently. This is a
      NumPy behaviour, not an OpenCV one, and it is why the lecture reaches for
      cv.absdiff on the very first line of the detector.

    
Where it hides
The wrapped frame does not look broken. It looks like a
      noisy difference image with a lot of motion in it, and a threshold applied on top of it
      returns a plausible-looking mask. The bug survives casual inspection.

    
What OpenCV does instead
cv.absdiff, cv.add,
      cv.subtract and friends saturate: they clip at 0 and 255 rather than
      wrapping. Mixing NumPy operators and OpenCV calls in one pipeline mixes two different
      overflow rules.

    
Measured here
On frames 30 and 41 of the highway clip, 24,998 of the
      57,600 pixels got darker. Thresholded at 10, the uint8 route reports 26,382
      moving pixels and cv.absdiff reports 3,634. Both numbers are computed in
      the page from the frames above.
