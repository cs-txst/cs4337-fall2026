Image Feature Extraction Demos

Five interactive pages for Image Feature Extraction, one for each place where the lecture states a parameter and then never varies it. Each page opens on its own, runs offline, and prints the OpenCV call behind every control.

Detect, describe, match

The pages follow the lecture's three stages in order. The first two are about choosing where and at what size to look; the last three are about turning a patch into numbers and deciding when two sets of numbers mean the same point.

They run offline

No network, no installs, no libraries. The photographs, the 3,347 measured SIFT matches and every line of code sit inside the file, so a copied folder or a memory stick behaves exactly the same on a classroom projector.

Measured, not asserted

The matches on the ratio-test page are cv2.SIFT_create output on the notebook's own photograph, graded against a transform that is known exactly. Where a page recomputes something to follow a slider, it was checked against OpenCV first.

Detection: where to look, and at what size

deck slides 17 to 57

Fastest Change, Slowest Change

Drag a window over a photograph and watch M, the largest and smallest amount the patch changes, the ellipse and the response map move with it. Every argument of cv2.cornerHarris is a slider, and the three scores are a toggle.

slides 17 to 30 blockSize, sigma, kthree scores
TRY: park the window on a corner, then push blockSize to 21. Watch the reading turn into an edge without a single pixel of the photo changing.

Choosing Sigma From the Image

The same field of sunflowers at full size and at three quarters. Click a flower and the two response curves peak at different sigma, and the ratio of the peaks recovers the size change.

slides 35 to 57 sigma laddernormalization toggle
TRY: predict the ratio of the two peaks before you click. Then switch the sigma squared normalization off and see what the ratio becomes.

Description: turning a patch into numbers

deck slides 67 to 78

Cells, Blocks and 3,780

A tennis player cropped to exactly the 64 by 128 window OpenCV's person detector expects. Hover a cell for its nine-bin histogram, and take the descriptor length apart into the numbers that produce it.

slides 68 to 72 cell, block, stride, binssigned toggle
TRY: the lecture says 324 and the notebook prints 3,780. Set the sliders so the readout gives each one, and see what had to change.

Turning the Patch Straight

Turn one patch with a dial. A raw pixel list stops matching at once, a gradient histogram is worse, and the same histogram taken after the patch is turned upright barely moves at all.

slides 67, 73 to 78 rotation dialthree descriptors
TRY: a histogram already ignores where each gradient sat in the patch. Predict whether that alone survives rotation, then watch the grey curve.

Matching: deciding what counts as the same point

deck slides 84 to 88

Why 0.75

All 3,347 SIFT descriptors of a photograph matched against the same photograph at half size, where the right answer is known exactly. Move Lowe's threshold and grade it: precision, recall, and the two populations it separates.

slides 84 to 88 ratio slider3,347 real matches
TRY: set the threshold to 1.00, which switches the test off entirely. Guess what fraction of nearest neighbors are right before you look.

Where the images come from

The sunflowers, the church, the tennis court and the barbet are all files the accompanying notebook already loads, so anything seen here can be reproduced by running one cell against the same picture.

What these are for

Slides are static and most of this lecture is a function from pixels to numbers. The gap a deck leaves is not what the result looks like but what happens as the knob turns, and that is the only thing these pages do.

Reading the code bar

Every page ends with the OpenCV calls its controls stand for, with the values the sliders are currently on highlighted. Copy it into the notebook and the same thing happens there.
