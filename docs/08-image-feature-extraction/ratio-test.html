Why 0.75

Every one of the 3,347 SIFT descriptors in a photograph, matched against the same photograph at half size. Because the second image is the first one scaled, the right answer for every match is known exactly, so the ratio test can be graded rather than argued about.

Why not an absolute distance
Slide the threshold to 1.00 and the test is switched off: every descriptor keeps its nearest neighbor, and only about a quarter of those are right. No fixed cutoff on the raw distance fixes that, because how far apart descriptors sit depends on the texture, the contrast and how crowded the scene is. The second-nearest distance measures that crowding for each feature separately, which is what makes the ratio self-calibrating.

    
Reading the two curves
Pushing the threshold up buys recall and sells precision, and the exchange rate is terrible above about 0.8. Going from 0.75 to 0.90 recovers a handful of extra correct matches and admits several hundred wrong ones. Lowe picked 0.75 because that is roughly where the curve turns, and it has held up for twenty years.

    
Wrong matches are not the end
Even at 0.75 a few percent of the surviving matches are wrong. The stage after this one throws most of them out: fit a single transform to all the matches with RANSAC and keep only the ones that agree with it. A wrong match has to be wrong in a way that happens to be consistent with every other match to survive that, which is rare.
