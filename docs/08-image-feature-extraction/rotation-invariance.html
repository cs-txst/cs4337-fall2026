Two different fixes, and only one works
A histogram of gradient orientations already ignores where in the patch each gradient came from, which is why it survives a small shift. It does not survive rotation, because turning the patch turns every gradient with it and the whole histogram slides sideways. The grey curve is exactly that: a descriptor that fixed the wrong problem. Turning the patch upright first is what fixes the right one.

    
Why the blue curve is not flat
Two effects, and both are worth knowing. The dominant direction is read off a histogram with 36 bins, so it starts out quantized to 10 degrees and is then refined by fitting a parabola through the peak. Land the dial on a multiple of 10 and the canonical patch reproduces the original exactly; in between it is turned by a degree or two too much. That is the sawtooth, and its period is exactly 10 degrees. The taller spikes, near 75, 165, 255 and 345 degrees, are something else: there the two strongest bins are close enough to trade places and the canonical frame jumps. Over the full turn the canonical descriptor sits at a median distance of 0.10, against 0.54 for the raw pixel list and 0.98 for the histogram that was never turned.

    
When there is no dominant direction
The method rests on the orientation histogram having one clear peak. On a patch with several comparable peaks, a small change in the image can flip which one wins, and the whole description jumps. SIFT does not gamble on it: when a second peak reaches 80 percent of the first, it emits a second keypoint at the same place with that orientation instead, and lets the matching stage decide between them.
