The Window That Climbs

1,200 pixels of the tennis court photo, placed by their color. Click anywhere to drop a window: it moves to the mean of the points inside it, again and again, until it stops on a peak of density. Every point whose climb ends on the same peak is one segment, and the window size alone decides how many peaks there are.

No K, but not no choice
Mean shift never asks how many segments you want, and that is its selling point. The bandwidth answers the question instead: on these 1,200 pixels a radius of 3 finds 51 peaks, 8 finds 5, anything from 12 to 20 finds 3, and 25 or more finds a single peak holding everything.

    
Why only 1,200 points
Every step of every climb averages the points inside a window, so running it from every point costs roughly points times points times steps. That is why the lecture calls mean shift expensive, and why OpenCV's image version, cv.pyrMeanShiftFiltering, limits each window to nearby pixels with a spatial radius sp as well as a color radius sr.

    
What runs here
The flat-window mean shift of scikit-learn's MeanShift: a climb from every point, stopping when a step moves less than a thousandth of the bandwidth; peaks closer than one bandwidth merged, the most crowded kept; each pixel of the photo then given its nearest peak. At each bandwidth listed on the left it finds the same number of peaks as scikit-learn on the same points.
