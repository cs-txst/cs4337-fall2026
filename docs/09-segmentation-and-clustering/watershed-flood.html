Flooding From Markers

Three overlapping disks threshold into a single white blob. Turn the distance to the background upside down and each disk becomes a valley; flood the valleys from markers, and a dam goes up wherever two floods meet. Raise the water, move the marker threshold, and add a little noise to see where watershed goes wrong.

One fraction for three sizes
The disks have radii 80, 62 and 48, and the largest distance in the blob is about 79. Keeping distance above 0.5 of that leaves one marker in each disk. Above about 0.6, the smallest disk never gets that far from its own edge, loses its marker and is flooded by its neighbor; below 0.4, the two left disks stay joined at the neck and share one marker.

    
Why every minimum is wrong
Without chosen markers, watershed floods from every local minimum. Even the clean disks have 7, because the distance transform has small flat spots along its ridges, so three disks become 7 regions. A quarter of a unit of noise makes it 22, half a unit 410, and one unit 2,140: every little dip floods its own region. That is the over-segmentation the lecture shows on a real photo.

    
What runs here
The distances are OpenCV's own cv.distanceTransform(thresh, cv.DIST_L2, 5) of the notebook's disks, and the markers are built exactly as the notebook builds them. The flood is a priority flood on the height, the algorithm skimage.segmentation.watershed(-dist, markers) runs; it finds the same number of regions as cv.watershed in the notebook at every threshold.
