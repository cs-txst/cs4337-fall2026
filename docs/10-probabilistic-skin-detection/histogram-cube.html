Inside the Color Histograms

The notebook's skin and non-skin histograms are 32 × 32 × 32 arrays: one number for each small cube of the RGB color cube. Here every non-empty bin is drawn where its colors live, in its own color, with its area showing its probability. How to read the cube
The dashed line joins the black and white corners. It is the gray axis, where R = G = B, and each corner is labeled with its color. Hue changes as you go around that axis and saturation grows as you move away from it. Skin sits in a narrow band on the red-orange side, from dark brown up to nearly white. Non-skin is spread through the whole cube but heaviest along the gray axis.

    
Where these two histograms come from
They are counts divided by their totals. The smallest non-zero bin of each is exactly one pixel, which gives the totals: 80,207,463 skin pixels and 854,296,711 non-skin pixels. Jones and Rehg (IJCV 2002) report 80,377,671 and 854,744,181 for the skin and non-skin models they built from 13,640 hand-labeled web photos, within 0.2% of these, so these are almost certainly 32-bin histograms of that same labeled set.

    
Coarser bins
Going from 32 to 16 bins per channel merges each 2 × 2 × 2 block of bins into one, adding their probabilities, so the total stays 1. Each bin then covers more colors, needs fewer training pixels to fill, and tells similar colors apart less well. The 32-bin version needs 32,768 numbers; the 4-bin version needs 64.
